Room Capture Protocol

Two-person field kit — 3BHK dimensional survey. Print this and carry it. Every number here is decided for you; do not improvise during the walk.

Your two jobs

PERSON A — CAMERAnever stops moving sideways
  • Holds the phone vertically, 1.40 m, camera level, on the 1× lens.
  • Performs every photo pass on page 3, in the order given.
  • Shoots continuously while walking. Do not stop to compose.
  • Photographs the measurement card each time B reads a number out.
  • Calls "clear" before shooting so B steps out of frame.
PERSON B — SURVEY & MEASUREnever in the photo
  • Before shooting: walk the room, count corners, doors, windows, mirrors and false ceiling. Call the list aloud so A knows the target count.
  • Lay the 1 m scale marker on the floor at the room start.
  • Stick masking-tape marks on the floor at every corner. These anchor the tape later.
  • Park out of frame during shooting, or leave the room entirely.
  • After shooting: measure with the 5 m tape, writing every number on the card.
  • Tick the acceptance checklist (page 6) before leaving the room.
  • Write the room name on the card before measuring. Never measure an unnamed room.

Why you cannot do both jobs at once

Shooting and measuring interfere: a tape left in frame pollutes the photo set, and a measuring posture breaks the walking rhythm the photo passes depend on. So the room cycle is always shoot first, measure second. Never interleave them.

How many rooms

Photographing is fast — about 12 minutes for a mid-size room. Measuring is slow — about 28 minutes. So capture every room you can reach, but only measure the top three. A room photographed but not measured still helps; a room measured but not photographed helps nothing at all.

The room cycle

Steps 1–5 for every room. Never skip step 1 — it is what stops you photographing a room with the fan spinning and a door half shut.

Step 1 — Make the room still B leads · 30 s

  • Ceiling fan OFF. Pedestal fan OFF. Air conditioning off.
  • Remove or push down loose cushions, clothes on chairs, movable floor objects.
  • Curtains fully open or fully closed — then do not touch them for this room.
  • Doors and windows open. You need the reveal and the true opening width.
  • Switch on every light in this room now and leave them on for the whole session.
  • No person, no pet, no tripod anywhere in frame.

Step 2 — Survey and count B leads · 60 s

Walk the perimeter once, calling out and counting:

  • Number of corners — write the number on the card
  • Every door and every window, with sill heights
  • Every mirror — flag each one; they corrupt depth silently
  • False / POP ceiling? If yes, note it — ceiling height becomes ambiguous
  • Any damage: cracks, chips, missing tiles, stains, water marks

These counts are A's shot targets. Without them A does not know when to stop.

Step 3 — Lay the anchors B does · 90 s

  • Lay 1.000 m of tape flat on the floor with a masking-tape mark at each end, near a corner but inside the room. This is the scale reference — do not skip it.
  • Stick a small masking-tape cross on the floor at each corner. These are the tape's anchor points and the photo-to-measurement correspondence.

Step 4 — Photograph A does · ~12 min

Run the eight passes on the next page, in order. B leaves the room or parks in a corner well out of frame. A starts at the scale marker and walks anticlockwise, one full loop per pass, so every pass covers the same walls in the same order — that is what makes gaps obvious.

Step 5 — Measure B leads · ~28 min

Tier 1 and Tier 2 rooms only — full list on page 5. A photographs the card with each number legible, filling the frame.

If you have only 90 minutes, cut in this order

  1. Living room: passes 1, 2, corners, openings, scale marker. Then measure it.
  2. Master bedroom: same. Then measure it.
  3. Stop there. Everything else is optional — two rooms done properly beats seven done badly.

Never photograph a room you will not measure, and never measure a room you did not photograph.

The three mistakes that cost the most

1. Panning in place. Standing in the middle and rotating the camera produces almost no geometric information — rotate-only motion is nearly degenerate. You must translate sideways. Passes 1 and 2 are the only passes that carry real geometry.

2. Stopping to frame each shot. Every stop-and-compose breaks the overlap chain and wastes the parallax of continuous motion. Walk steadily, shoot roughly every step, review afterwards.

3. Skipping the corners. Every shortcut gets taken at the corners, and corners are exactly what determine a room's shape. Budget the full 12 shots.

Lighting — pick your moment, do not fix it later

Best: overcast, or the hour after sunrise, or the last hour before sunset. Worst: bright sun through a west-facing window, which in an Andhra summer produces hard shadow edges and blown highlights no setting recovers.

  • Never have a window behind the wall you are shooting — the wall goes to silhouette.
  • Never let a person's or a fan's shadow fall across a wall you are shooting.
  • Turn off any flickering LED, any TV, and every moving fan before you begin.
  • If a room is dim, switch on its existing lights and stay. Do not chase better light — you will lose the schedule.

Four hazards that silently corrupt results

These do far more damage than any camera setting. Note each one's location: a named limitation is acceptable, a silent wrong number is not.

  1. Mirrors. Monocular depth models return a plausible, confident, wrong depth on a mirror instead of failing. Note every mirror. Never trust a measurement taken near one.
  2. Glass and balcony doors. Same failure, plus see-through geometry.
  3. POP / false ceiling. A dropped ceiling below the slab makes "ceiling height" ambiguous by 10–20 cm. Photograph both levels, and state which one you measured.
  4. Glossy surfaces — polished granite floor, glossy modular kitchen. Specular reflection leaves no usable texture. Photograph edges and matte areas only.

Painted featureless walls are the hardest common case — no texture to match on. Get a light switch, curtain rail, ladder or cornice into shots near bare wall; those are your texture anchors.

Ground truth — measuring with the 5 m tape

Tier 1 and Tier 2 rooms only. These numbers are the truth every other number is checked against, so precision matters more than completeness.

One rule, and it matters. The tape is your validation reference. The pipeline gets its scale from the 1 m marker photographed in the scene. Never calibrate the pipeline against these hand-measured numbers — that makes the benchmark circular and worthless, and a reviewer checks for exactly this.

Solo measuring technique

  • Use the masking-tape corner marks as anchors. Hook the tape's metal end on a mark, pull to the next mark, read, write, step. You never need a second hand at the far end.
  • For runs over 5 m, step the tape in marked segments and sum them. Mark every intermediate point so a later re-measure is reproducible.
  • Pull the tape taut and straight. A sagging tape reads short; a twisted one reads long. Sight along it.
  • Read at eye level, directly above the tape, never at an angle.
  • Record millimetres, not centimetres, with leading zeros: 1245, not 1 245.
  • Measure every wall length twice, independently, and write both numbers. If they differ by more than 5 mm, measure a third time and keep all three.

Acceptance checklist — tick before leaving each room

B owns this page. Do not move to the next room until every line is ticked or explicitly given up. A room photographed badly is a lost room — you will not come back for it.

Scene

  • Fan off, AC off, no moving person, no pet
  • Doors and windows open
  • Curtains fully open or fully closed, and untouched since
  • All room lights on

Coverage

  • Pass 1 complete — full loop at 1.0 m, spacing 0.30 m
  • Pass 2 complete — second loop from the opposite wall at 1.8 m
  • All four corners shot — 3 photos each, standing inside the corner
  • Floor-to-wall and ceiling-to-wall tilts at each corner
  • Floor pass done
  • Ceiling pass done, or POP ceiling photographed at both levels
  • Every door photographed — 5–6 photos each, square-on, full height
  • Every window photographed — 5–6 photos each
  • Every feature and every damage item photographed at 0.5–0.8 m

Anchors — the two things that cannot be redone later

  • 1.000 m scale marker on the floor, photographed in 3 frames with the tape legible
  • Masking-tape corner marks placed and their positions noted

Ground truth — Tier 1 and Tier 2 only

  • Room name on the card before measuring
  • All ~25 measurements written in millimetres
  • Every wall length measured twice, both numbers written
  • Opening widths taken at top, middle and bottom
  • False ceiling noted, and which height was measured stated explicitly
  • Every mirror and glass surface located and noted
  • Card photographed filling the frame

Technical

  • Zero photos taken on 0.6× ultra-wide or 2× zoom
  • EXIF preserved — no screenshots, no re-saving, nothing sent through WhatsApp or Instagram
  • First photo of the room checked for correct orientation
  • Photo count matches the expected pass count — 0 means a broken loop, 300 means composing instead of sweeping

Wrap-up

Troubleshooting for when the session goes sideways, and the checks to run before you leave the building.

If something goes wrong

  • Fan will not stop, curtain will not stay — photograph anyway, note it, move on. Do not lose the schedule.
  • Room is far too dark — turn on its lights and continue. Never use flash.
  • You are running late — drop passes 5 and 6 (floor and ceiling) first, then shorten pass 2 to a half loop. Never drop corners or openings.
  • A wall is blank painted plaster — get the switchboard, cornice, skirting or door frame into shots near it. Those anchors are what make it reconstructable.
  • You find damage worth documenting well — spend the extra 2 minutes. Take 5 overlapping close shots plus one wide shot for context, with a tape in frame for scale.

Before you leave the flat — final 10-minute check

  • Photo count is roughly what you expected (~600 for the full flat)
  • Every measured room has its card photographed with the room name legible
  • The 1 m scale marker was photographed in at least two rooms
  • Photos were not sent through WhatsApp, Instagram or any compressor
  • Everything backed up to a second location before you leave
  • You roughly know how many photos each room has, in case a set needs re-shooting

Hard cutoff

If you reach 13:00 and the Tier 1 rooms are not finished, stop photographing new rooms and start measuring what you already have. A measured room you photographed beats an unmeasured room you did not. Everything below that line can be recorded as a known gap — and an honest gap costs far less than a broken pipeline.
